# Safety assessment

Magnesium has a wide margin of safety in healthy people and a narrow one in kidney
disease. This document works through each risk, the evidence behind it, and the
practical implication.

## 1. Summary

| Risk | Who | Severity | Likelihood at recommended doses | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diarrhoea, nausea, cramping | Anyone | Low | Common above 300–400 mg elemental, form-dependent | Split doses, take with food, switch form |
| Hypermagnesemia | Kidney impairment, very high doses | High | Low in healthy kidneys | Avoid in CKD without supervision |
| Drug interaction | Antibiotic, bisphosphonate, diuretic and PPI users | Moderate | Common if unspaced | Separate doses; monitor |
| Hypotension/bradycardia | IV or very high oral doses | High | Very low orally | Clinical setting only |
| Fetal/neonatal effects | IV magnesium in pregnancy | High | Not applicable to oral supplements | Obstetric supervision |

## 2. Gastrointestinal effects

### What happens

Unabsorbed magnesium is osmotically active: it holds water in the bowel, speeding
transit and loosening stools. This is the mechanism behind magnesium-based laxatives
and the most common reason people stop supplements.

### Which forms

Carbonate, chloride, gluconate and oxide are most often reported to cause diarrhoea
(R01). Citrate loosens the bowel dose-dependently. Glycinate is generally gentler,
but large doses still cause loose stools in some people.

### Dose relationship

The effect is driven by the amount of unabsorbed magnesium, not the salt's identity
alone. A 400 mg dose of a poorly absorbed form delivers more magnesium to the colon
than a 200 mg dose of a soluble one. Splitting the dose reduces the osmotic load per
administration.

### Management

- Reduce the dose.
- Split it into two or three smaller doses.
- Take with food.
- Switch to bisglycinate or a lower dose of citrate.
- If diarrhoea persists, stop and look for another cause.

Diarrhoea is not "detox". It is a dose-limiting adverse effect.

## 3. Hypermagnesemia

### Definition and symptoms

Hypermagnesemia is a serum magnesium above about 1.05 mmol/L (2.6 mg/dL). Symptoms
usually appear at 1.74–2.61 mmol/L and include hypotension, nausea, vomiting,
flushing, urinary retention, ileus, depression, lethargy, muscle weakness,
hyporeflexia, respiratory depression, arrhythmia and cardiac arrest (R01, R45).

Deep tendon reflexes disappear near 5 mmol/L; cardiac arrest can occur above
6–7.5 mmol/L (R45).

### Who is at risk

- **Chronic kidney disease**, especially eGFR below 30.
- **Acute kidney injury**, particularly the oliguric phase.
- **Dialysis patients**, where serum magnesium tracks dialysate concentration.
- **People taking very high doses** of magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids.
- **Older adults**, in whom kidney function and polypharmacy coincide.

A retrospective cohort of 320 patients prescribed magnesium oxide found that 23%
developed some degree of hypermagnesemia. Independent risk factors were eGFR
≤55.4 mL/min (OR 3.1), magnesium oxide ≥1650 mg/day (OR 1.9) and duration ≥36 days
(OR 2.2) (R43).

### Documented cases

Published fatalities include a woman with end-stage renal disease taking 3000 mg/day
of magnesium hydroxide for constipation (serum 9.9 mg/dL, recovered with dialysis)
and a woman with normal renal function who reached 11.0 mg/dL and died after cardiac
arrest (R44). Both involved high-dose hydroxide, not a standard supplement dose.

### Why healthy kidneys are protected

The kidneys filter and excrete excess magnesium efficiently. That is why food
magnesium has no upper limit and why the UL applies only to supplements and
medications. The protection disappears when renal function falls.

## 4. Kidney disease

This is the central safety issue. The risk is **accumulation**, not direct
nephrotoxicity. Magnesium does not damage healthy kidneys.

| eGFR | Practical position |
| --- | --- |
| ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m² | Standard supplement doses are generally safe |
| 30–59 | Use only with clinician advice; monitor serum magnesium |
| <30 | Avoid unless prescribed and monitored |
| Dialysis | Individualised; dietary and dialysate magnesium are managed by the renal team |

Magnesium is also of research interest in CKD for vascular calcification, but that
is a clinical research question, not a reason for self-supplementation (R42, R54).

## 5. Cardiovascular effects

Oral magnesium at recommended doses does not cause clinically significant
hypotension or bradycardia in healthy people. At very high serum levels, magnesium
acts as a calcium antagonist and depresses cardiac conduction, which is the basis of
its use in eclampsia and the danger in overdose. This is a clinical-setting risk,
not a supplement risk at normal doses.

## 6. Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Magnesium requirements rise during pregnancy (350–360 mg/day total). Oral magnesium
is generally considered safe within the UL. High-dose oral magnesium has not been
shown to prevent preeclampsia; intravenous magnesium sulfate is a clinical treatment
for seizure prophylaxis and is monitored in hospital.

Breastfeeding requirements are 310–320 mg/day total. Standard supplement doses are
compatible; high doses should be discussed with a clinician.

## 7. Children

Children have lower ULs: 65 mg at 1–3 years, 110 mg at 4–8 years, 350 mg at 9–18
years. Paediatric doses should be based on age and weight, using products designed
for children. Overdose in children is a recognised risk with magnesium-containing
laxatives.

## 8. Older adults

Two forces act in opposite directions: lower intake and reduced absorption increase
deficiency risk, while declining kidney function increases accumulation risk. The
practical approach is to check kidney function, start low, and review regularly.

## 9. Drug interactions

### Antibiotics

Magnesium forms insoluble complexes with tetracyclines (doxycycline, demeclocycline)
and quinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), reducing antibiotic absorption. Take
magnesium at least 2 hours before or 4–6 hours after (R01).

### Bisphosphonates

Magnesium-rich supplements reduce the absorption of oral bisphosphonates such as
alendronate. Separate by at least 2 hours (R01).

### Diuretics

Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) and thiazides increase urinary magnesium
loss and can deplete magnesium. Potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride,
spironolactone) reduce excretion and raise the risk of excess (R01).

### Proton pump inhibitors

Long-term PPI use (typically more than a year) can cause hypomagnesemia. In the
cases FDA reviewed, magnesium supplements raised low levels in most patients, but
about 25% needed to stop the PPI. FDA advises checking magnesium before and during
long-term PPI therapy (R41).

### Zinc and calcium

Very high zinc intake (142 mg/day) interfered with magnesium absorption and balance
in a controlled study (R01). High-dose calcium can compete with magnesium
absorption; separate large doses. No fixed calcium-to-magnesium ratio is required.

## 10. Overdose

Oral overdose in a person with healthy kidneys usually produces diarrhoea rather
than systemic toxicity, because absorption is limited and the kidneys excrete the
rest. Serious toxicity typically requires either massive doses of laxatives or
antacids, or kidney impairment.

**Signs of serious toxicity:** severe hypotension, bradycardia, loss of deep tendon
reflexes, respiratory depression, confusion, coma, cardiac arrest.

**Action:** stop all magnesium-containing products and seek emergency care. Calcium
gluconate is the antidote in hospital; dialysis removes magnesium in kidney failure.

## 11. Monitoring

Monitor serum magnesium if:

- eGFR is below 60;
- the patient is on dialysis;
- the patient takes a PPI long-term, a diuretic, or both;
- the dose exceeds 350 mg/day;
- symptoms suggest deficiency or excess.

Remember that serum magnesium is a poor marker of tissue status (R01, R51). A
normal level does not exclude depletion, and a high level in kidney disease is a
warning sign that requires action.

## 12. Bottom line

For a healthy adult with normal kidney function, magnesium at 100–350 mg/day
supplemental is low-risk. The main nuisance is diarrhoea. The main danger is kidney
disease, where the same dose can accumulate. The main interaction risk is with
antibiotics and bisphosphonates, which is managed by spacing doses. There is no
meaningful risk of "magnesium toxicity" from food.
