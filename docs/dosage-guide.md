# Dosage guide

This guide translates the evidence into practical protocols. It is not
individualised medical advice. People with kidney disease, people taking
prescription medication, and pregnant or breastfeeding women should involve a
clinician.

## 2. Upper limit

The tolerable upper intake level for **supplemental and medicinal** magnesium is
**350 mg/day for adults**. It does not include food magnesium, because healthy
kidneys excrete the excess (R01). For children: 65 mg at 1–3 years, 110 mg at 4–8
years, 350 mg at 9–18 years.

Exceeding the UL is not automatically dangerous, but it is the point at which
diarrhoea becomes common and the risk of accumulation in impaired kidneys rises.
Higher doses (for migraine, for example) should be clinician-supervised.

## 3. General supplementation protocol

**Who:** adults with low dietary intake, or with a condition or medication that
increases loss or reduces absorption.

**Dose:** 100–200 mg elemental magnesium per dose, once or twice daily. Total
supplemental intake at or below 350 mg/day unless supervised.

**Form:** citrate or bisglycinate. Oxide if cost is the constraint and stools are
tolerable.

**Timing:** with a meal to reduce stomach upset; evening if sleep is the goal;
separate from interacting drugs (section 7).

**Titration:** start at 100–200 mg/day for one week. Increase only if needed and
tolerated. Loose stools are the dose-limiting effect.

**Review:** reassess at 4–8 weeks. If there is no clear benefit and intake is
adequate, stop.

## 4. Protocols by goal

### 4.1 General adequacy

- 100–200 mg/day of citrate or bisglycinate.
- Better: increase dietary intake — pumpkin seeds (156 mg/oz), chia (111 mg/oz),
  almonds (80 mg/oz), spinach (78 mg per half cup), cashews (74 mg/oz), black beans
  (60 mg per half cup), edamame (50 mg per half cup) (R01).
- A supplement is a top-up, not a substitute for food.

### 4.2 Sleep

- 200–400 mg elemental magnesium in the evening, as bisglycinate or citrate.
- Trials used 250–500 mg/day for 4–8 weeks (R15–R18).
- Expect a small effect at best; the pooled sleep-onset improvement was about 17
  minutes in older adults, with low certainty.
- Do not use magnesium to treat chronic insomnia without addressing sleep hygiene,
  caffeine, alcohol and CBT-I.

### 4.3 Anxiety and stress

- 200–300 mg/day, often divided.
- Evidence is weak and heterogeneous; the best trial enrolled adults with low serum
  magnesium and high stress (R26).
- Magnesium plus vitamin B6 may add a small benefit in severe stress, but the
  combination is not required for absorption.
- Not a substitute for therapy or medication in an anxiety disorder.

### 4.4 Migraine prevention

- 600 mg/day of trimagnesium dicitrate, divided into two doses (R19, R21).
- This exceeds the 350 mg supplemental UL and requires clinician supervision.
- Expect loose stools; 18.6% of trial participants had diarrhoea.
- Allow 8–12 weeks before judging effect.
- Magnesium is not an acute migraine treatment.

### 4.5 Blood pressure

- 300–370 mg/day for 8–12 weeks (R22, R23).
- Expect about 2 mmHg systolic reduction overall, and up to about 7.7 mmHg in
  treated hypertensives.
- Continue prescribed antihypertensives; magnesium is adjunctive at best.
- Monitor blood pressure rather than assuming benefit.

### 4.6 Muscle cramps

- Not recommended for idiopathic cramps. The pooled effect is about 0.18 cramps per
  week, not clinically meaningful (R11).
- Investigate causes: dehydration, electrolyte loss, diuretics, kidney disease,
  alcohol, hypothyroidism, peripheral neuropathy, and medications such as statins.
- Pregnancy cramps: evidence is inconsistent; a low-risk trial is reasonable with
  obstetric advice.

### 4.7 Exercise and recovery

- No ergogenic benefit in replete athletes. One trial found a small performance
  decrement (R32).
- If an athlete is deficient or has low intake, correct it; that is a different
  claim.
- Soreness may be modestly reduced (R30, R31), but this is not a reason to take a
  high dose.

### 4.8 Constipation

- 200–400 mg of magnesium oxide or citrate as needed.
- This is an osmotic laxative effect, not magnesium repletion.
- Do not use chronically at high doses without medical advice, especially with any
  degree of kidney impairment.
- Adequate fluid and fibre remain the first-line approach.

## 5. Timing and splitting

- Absorption is saturable, so **two 200 mg doses beat one 400 mg dose** (R08).
- Take with food if it causes nausea.
- Evening dosing is traditional for sleep; the evidence for timing is thin.
- Separate from antibiotics and bisphosphonates (section 7).

## 6. Special populations

### Older adults

Lower intake, reduced absorption and more medications. Start at 100 mg/day and
titrate. Check kidney function before long-term use, because eGFR declines with age
and hypermagnesemia risk rises.

### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Requirements rise to 350–360 mg/day total. Oral magnesium has not been shown to
prevent preeclampsia or reliably prevent cramps. Do not exceed the UL without
obstetric advice.

### Children

Doses are age- and weight-specific, and the UL is much lower. Use paediatric
products and medical advice; do not scale adult doses by eye.

### Chronic kidney disease

Do not self-supplement. Magnesium excretion falls with eGFR, and accumulation can
cause dangerous hypermagnesemia. Decisions require a nephrologist, especially on
dialysis (R42, R54).

### People taking diuretics or PPIs

Loop and thiazide diuretics increase magnesium loss; long-term PPIs reduce
absorption and can cause hypomagnesemia (R41). These are reasons to check
magnesium, not to self-prescribe high doses.

## 7. Drug spacing

| Drug | Minimum separation |
| --- | --- |
| Tetracycline antibiotics | Magnesium 2 h before or 4–6 h after |
| Quinolone antibiotics | Magnesium 2 h before or 4–6 h after |
| Oral bisphosphonates | At least 2 h |
| Levothyroxine | Separate by 4 h (general mineral precaution) |
| High-dose zinc | Separate doses; avoid chronic 142 mg/day zinc |

Sources: R01 for antibiotics and bisphosphonates. The levothyroxine spacing is a
general mineral-absorption precaution rather than a magnesium-specific trial.

## 8. Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loose stools | Osmotic load, especially oxide or citrate | Lower dose, split it, switch to bisglycinate |
| Nausea | Taking on an empty stomach | Take with food |
| No effect on sleep | Dose too low, or effect is small | Reassess; do not escalate indefinitely |
| No effect on cramps | Magnesium is not effective for idiopathic cramps | Investigate other causes |
| Constipation instead of loosening | Low dose or oxide in a constipated person | Increase fluid and fibre; review dose |

## 9. Stopping

Magnesium is not a drug that requires tapering. If intake is adequate and there is
no clear benefit after 4–8 weeks, stop. If it was correcting a documented
deficiency or a drug-induced loss, continue under the treating clinician's plan.
