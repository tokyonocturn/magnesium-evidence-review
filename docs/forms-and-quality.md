# Forms and product quality

This document explains what the different magnesium salts are, how much magnesium
they actually contain, what the absorption evidence shows, and how to judge a
finished product. The full chemistry table is generated in
[`generated/forms-chemistry.md`](generated/forms-chemistry.md).

## 1. Why the salt matters at all

Magnesium is always bound to something. The counter-ion determines:

- how much elemental magnesium a gram of powder contains;
- how soluble the salt is in the gut;
- whether it draws water into the bowel (osmotic laxative effect);
- whether the counter-ion has its own biological activity (taurine, glycine,
  malate, threonate);
- how bulky the pill has to be.

It does **not** determine whether magnesium works as a mineral. Once absorbed, the
magnesium ion is the same. Claims that a particular salt "targets" an organ are
almost always mechanistic extrapolations, not clinical findings.

The practical consequence: **a 500 mg capsule of a bulky salt cannot contain 500 mg
of magnesium.** At best it contains about 300 mg (oxide) and at worst about 30 mg
(gluconate).

### Hydration is a hidden variable

Magnesium chloride is 25.5% magnesium anhydrous but 12.0% as the hexahydrate.
Magnesium sulfate is 20.2% anhydrous but 9.9% as the heptahydrate. Magnesium
citrate is 16.2% as the trimagnesium salt and 11.3% as the monobasic form. A label
that says only "magnesium citrate" leaves a 30% uncertainty in the dose.

### The threonate case

Commercial Magtein is labelled as 2000 mg magnesium L-threonate yielding 144 mg
elemental magnesium, which is 7.2%. The anhydrous salt is 8.3%. Both figures are
plausible depending on hydration and assay; either way, **threonate is a low-dose
magnesium product**. A 2 g serving delivers about a third of the RDA.

## 3. Solubility and absorption

Solubility is not bioavailability, but it is a useful first approximation.

| Salt | Solubility in water | Absorption evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium oxide | 0.010 g/100 mL | ~4% |
| Magnesium citrate | ~20 g/100 mL | Higher than oxide; absolute % uncertain |
| Magnesium chloride | ~54 g/100 mL | 9–11% |
| Magnesium sulfate | ~35 g/100 mL | Oral laxative; transdermal negligible |
| Magnesium bisglycinate | ~1.36 g/100 mL | Equal to oxide overall in one trial; better in malabsorbers |
| Magnesium malate | Slightly soluble | No human head-to-head |
| Magnesium taurate | Slightly soluble | No human head-to-head |
| Magnesium threonate | Soluble | Animal brain uptake; human unmeasured |

Values from R04 and R10.

### What the trials actually compared

- **Oxide versus soluble salts.** Oxide 4% versus 9–11% for chloride, aspartate and
  lactate (R03). Oxide was only 43% soluble at peak gastric acidity (R04).
- **Citrate versus oxide over 60 days.** Citrate produced the highest serum
  magnesium; oxide did not differ from placebo (R05). The absolute urinary
  difference in a later saturation study was only 13.7 mg/day (R06).
- **Glycinate versus oxide in malabsorption.** Identical overall (23.5% vs 22.8%),
  better only in the four worst absorbers (R07).
- **Organic versus inorganic, generally.** Reviews find small or no differences
  among soluble salts (R08, R09).

**Conclusion:** choose a soluble salt; do not pay a large premium for a specific
organic salt on absorption grounds. The exception is malabsorption, where glycinate
has the only direct supporting data.

## 4. Form-by-form

### Magnesium oxide

The most concentrated common form and the cheapest per milligram. Poorly soluble,
poorly absorbed, and the most likely to cause diarrhoea. It is the active ingredient
in many OTC laxatives and antacids at 400–500 mg per tablet. Several positive
clinical trials used oxide (sleep in older adults, migraine comparator studies), so
"useless" is wrong; "poorly absorbed" is right. Best used for constipation or when
cost is the binding constraint.

### Magnesium citrate

Soluble, well absorbed, and the form with the most consistent bioavailability data.
It loosens the bowel dose-dependently, which is a feature for constipation and a
limitation for supplementation. It is the form used in the positive migraine trial
(as trimagnesium dicitrate) and in most blood-pressure trials. The best default for
general use.

### Magnesium bisglycinate (glycinate)

Magnesium bound to two glycine molecules. Moderate solubility, generally gentle on
the gut, and popular for sleep and anxiety. The only direct comparison with oxide
found no overall absorption advantage; the advantage appeared in malabsorbers
(R07). Glycine itself has sleep-related effects, but the doses used in magnesium
products are modest and the combination has not been shown to beat magnesium alone.

### Magnesium glycinate chelate (Albion TRAACS and similar)

Fully reacted chelates with documented manufacturing. Absorption is good and
tolerability is good, but there is no adequate head-to-head trial against citrate in
healthy adults. Much of the "6× better absorbed" marketing compares against oxide or
uses a proprietary assay that is not independently replicated.

### Magnesium malate

Magnesium bound to malic acid, a Krebs cycle intermediate. The combination is
marketed for fatigue and fibromyalgia. Malate is only slightly soluble, elemental
content is 12.6–15.5%, and there are no human head-to-head absorption trials. The
fibromyalgia literature is old and small.

### Magnesium taurate

Magnesium bound to taurine, marketed for cardiovascular health. Elemental content
is only 8.9%, so a 400 mg dose requires about 4.5 g of salt. There are no outcome
trials of the salt. Taurine has its own cardiovascular literature at gram doses;
that is not evidence for magnesium taurate.

### Magnesium L-threonate

A salt of magnesium and L-threonic acid. Animal studies show increased brain
magnesium (R34). Human trials measure cognition, not brain magnesium, and are small,
short and mostly industry-funded (R33–R36). Elemental content is 7.2–8.3%, the
highest cost per milligram in this sample, and the cognitive claims are unproven.

### Magnesium chloride

Highly soluble. Absorption is comparable to or better than oxide (R03). It is often
sold as a liquid, a "magnesium oil" (which is not an oil), or in sustained-release
tablets. The hexahydrate is only 12.0% magnesium, so doses are bulky. Oral chloride
is a reasonable choice; topical chloride is not a reliable delivery route.

### Magnesium sulfate (Epsom salt)

The heptahydrate is 9.9% magnesium and highly soluble. Oral use is as a laxative.
Transdermal absorption across intact skin is negligible (R38–R40). Baths may be
pleasant and may help relaxation, but they are not a magnesium-repletion strategy.

### Magnesium lactate, aspartate, orotate, gluconate

Lactate and aspartate are soluble and were used in older comparative studies with
absorption in the 9–11% range. Orotate is marketed for heart failure on the basis of
small, old trials; gluconate is bulky and mainly used where chloride is unsuitable.
None has a compelling advantage over citrate for general use.

## 5. Reading a label

### The five checks

1. **Elemental magnesium**, not compound weight. "Magnesium 200 mg (as magnesium
   glycinate)" is clear. "Magnesium glycinate 1000 mg" is not a dose.
2. **Serving size.** Front-of-pack numbers often refer to two tablets or a scoop.
3. **Form specificity.** "Magnesium citrate" without hydration is ambiguous;
   "trimagnesium dicitrate" is specific.
4. **Other actives.** Blends with B6, taurine, Malate or herbal "absorption" bases
   make attribution impossible.
5. **Certification.** A seal is a quality signal, not an efficacy signal.

### Red flags

- Front label "500 mg" with a supplement facts panel showing 100 mg elemental.
- "Magnesium complex" with no per-salt amounts.
- "99% absorption" or "10× more bioavailable" with no citation.
- "Magnesium oil" sold as a supplement.
- Claims about "brain penetration", "cellular detox" or "adrenal support".
- A dose above 350 mg per serving presented as a normal daily dose.

## 6. Third-party testing and certification

| Programme | What it verifies | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NSF Certified for Sport | Batch-level contaminants plus prohibited substances | Athletes subject to doping control |
| NSF/ANSI 173 (Contents Certified) | Identity, strength, contaminant limits, GMP | General quality assurance |
| USP Verified | Identity, strength, purity, dissolution, manufacturing | General quality assurance |
| Informed Sport | Batch testing for prohibited substances | Athletes |
| Informed Choice | Retail monitoring, not batch release | Weakest of the group |

Certification does not tell you whether the dose is sensible, whether the form is
appropriate, or whether the product is good value. It answers a narrower question:
is what is in the bottle what the label says, without unacceptable contaminants?

Independent testing has found real problems. ConsumerLab declined to approve two
magnesium products in 2024 because the form detected did not match the label (R46).
A peer-reviewed survey found only 41.3% of magnesium supplements within acceptable
deviation of their declared content, and only two samples matched exactly (R48).
Heavy-metal testing by independent labs has found lead in some products, including
brands marketed as clean; those results are product- and batch-specific and are not
used here as quantitative evidence.

## 7. Formats

| Format | Cost per mg | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Powder | Lowest | Flexible dosing, cheap | Measuring errors, taste, flavoured products add sweeteners |
| Tablets | Low–moderate | Compact, cheap | Often oxide-heavy, hard to swallow at high dose |
| Capsules | Moderate–high | Convenient, well labelled | Capsule size limits dose, many capsules per serving |
| Gummies | Highest | Palatable | Low elemental dose, sugar, poor value |

## 8. Practical quality checklist

- [ ] Elemental magnesium is stated per serving and per capsule/tablet.
- [ ] The salt is named specifically.
- [ ] The serving size is realistic for the dose you want.
- [ ] No hidden actives that matter to you.
- [ ] A certification mark if contaminants or sport are a concern.
- [ ] Cost per 400 mg elemental magnesium is within the normal range.
- [ ] The dose per serving is at or below 350 mg unless a clinician has advised
      otherwise.
